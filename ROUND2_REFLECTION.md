## 1. What was the most uncomfortable trade-off you made because of the time pressure?
The hardest trade-off was choosing a manual pricing-change trigger instead of a scheduled job. A scheduled workflow would feel more complete and more production-like, especially because the assignment explicitly mentions cron as an option. The reason I cut it was not that I thought it was unimportant, but that the assignment’s real requirement is reliable detection, notification, and rerun behavior. In this repo, a scheduler would have pulled time into deployment differences, provider limitations, route authentication, and “did the schedule actually run?” debugging. That risk was much larger than the product value of the schedule itself inside 36 hours. I decided it was better to ship a plain `POST /api/detect-changes` endpoint that a reviewer can call deterministically, verify end to end, and understand quickly. The uncomfortable part is that this choice is less automated than I would want in a production launch, but it was the right call for shipping the highest-confidence core behavior first.

## 2. If we extended the deadline by another 24 hours right now, what's the first thing you'd do?
The first thing I would do is lock down `POST /api/detect-changes` with authentication and then put it behind a real scheduler. That is the single highest-leverage follow-up because the detection logic already exists and works, but operationally it is still too exposed and too manual. I would prefer a small secret-based check for the route, then a scheduler that hits it on a known cadence. After that, I would add a lightweight audit log around the job so a reviewer or operator could see when detection last ran, how many audits were scanned, how many users were affected, and whether any email sends failed. I would not start with unsubscribe or dashboard work before that. The current product path proves the feature, but the very next step should be to make the trigger safe and automatic rather than adding more surface area around a still-manual operational path.

## 3. Looking back at your Round 1 codebase as a now-experienced user of it: what's one thing your Round 1 self made harder for your Round 2 self?
Round 1 made pricing too implicit inside the audit engine. The recommendation logic read directly from the shared pricing constant, which was convenient when the app only needed a one-time calculation, but it made Round 2 materially harder because replaying an old audit requires the exact opposite design. I needed the engine to accept “pricing as data” rather than “pricing as a global.” That single design choice created extra refactoring across the pricing service, audit service, persistence shape, and re-audit comparison logic. If I were revisiting Round 1, I would still keep the pricing catalog local and explicit, but I would make snapshot injection part of the calculation API from day one. That would have turned Round 2 from “refactor then build” into mostly “wire the new workflow.” The code separation in Round 1 helped me a lot; the main thing that slowed me down was not component structure, it was the hidden dependency on current pricing.
